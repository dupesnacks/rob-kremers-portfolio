I. Regulatory Framework & Cosmetic vs. Drug Classification

A. What is a Cosmetic?

Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic (FD&C) Act (21 U.S.C. § 321(i)), a cosmetic is:

"An article intended to be rubbed, poured, sprinkled, or sprayed on, introduced into, or otherwise applied to the human body or any part thereof for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, or altering the appearance."

Key test: Cosmetics have no therapeutic intent. They are intended for external use only to alter appearance or cleanse—not to treat, cure, or prevent disease, or affect body structure/function.

B. What is a Drug?

A drug is an article intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease, or to affect body structure or function. Therapeutic claims trigger drug classification.

C. GHK-Cu Positioning: Cosmetic (Not Drug)

GHK-Cu is a copper tripeptide that has published research supporting skincare benefits. However, we will position it strictly as cosmetic by:

  • Using only appearance-focused claims: "Helps skin look smoother," "improves the appearance of fine lines"
  • Avoiding therapeutic language: No "stimulates collagen," "heals wounds," "treats acne," "reduces inflammation"
  • Explicit topical-only positioning: "External use only"
  • Avoiding peptide/injectable framing: Position as "copper peptide skincare ingredient," not "peptide therapy" or "research peptide"
🔶 Medium Risk: Claims Drift

Scenario: Marketing copy inadvertently uses phrases like "stimulates natural collagen production" or "repairs skin" = FTC/FDA violation.

Mitigation: All marketing copy review required through CLAIMS_GUARDRAIL.md review process before publishing. Legal review of landing page, emails, ads, social posts recommended quarterly.

D. FDA Compliance Requirements for Cosmetics

Requirement What You Must Do Timeline
Facility Registration Register manufacturing facility with FDA if you manufacture in-house. If contract manufacturer (outsourced), they register; you ensure compliance. Before launch
Cosmetic Product Listing Submit product information to FDA (ingredient list, manufacturer, intended use). Optional but recommended for compliance credibility. Before/at launch
Ingredient Restrictions Comply with 21 CFR Part 700 (banned/restricted ingredients). GHK-Cu is NOT banned; copper tripeptide is allowed in cosmetics. Supplier verification
Labeling Requirements Product label must list ingredients in INCI format, net quantity, manufacturer, warnings (e.g., "For external use only"). Keep copies on file. Before packaging
Adverse Event Reporting Report serious adverse events (injuries, allergic reactions) to FDA within 15 days. Maintain complaint log. Ongoing (upon receipt)
Note: Unlike drugs, cosmetics do NOT require FDA pre-approval. FDA review is post-market (reactive). However, FTC can issue warning letters or enforcement actions if claims are unsubstantiated or misleading.

II. FTC Advertising Standards & Claims Substantiation

A. The FTC Endorsement Guides & Truth in Advertising

The FTC enforces the Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255), which require:

  • Truthfulness: All claims must be true and not misleading.
  • Substantiation: Before making a claim, you must have competent, reliable scientific evidence supporting it.
  • Disclosures: Material connections (e.g., influencer receives product for free) must be clearly disclosed.
  • Testimonials: Influencer testimonials must reflect typical consumer experience, not cherry-picked results.

B. Substantiation Standard for Skincare Claims

For a claim like "Improves the appearance of fine lines," you must have:

  • At least ONE competent, reliable clinical study (gold standard: randomized, controlled, published in peer-reviewed journal)
  • OR consumer perception data (blind testing, before/after photos rated by independent evaluators)
  • OR recognized expert opinion (dermatologist testimonial with disclosed conflict of interest)

Cost to substantiate: $10–50k for a credible clinical trial (external lab). Budget this in Phase 1–2.

🔶 High Risk: Unsubstantiated Claims

Scenario: You claim "Improves appearance of fine lines" with no clinical study. Competitor or FTC challenges you = cease-and-desist letter, forced corrective advertising ($50k+), potential fine.

Mitigation: Commission a clinical study with 3rd-party lab OR obtain peer-reviewed literature supporting the claim. Keep documentation in a "claims file" for FTC audits.

C. Influencer & Affiliate Marketing Rules

Scenario FTC Requirement Example Disclosure
Influencer receives free product Material connection must be disclosed (clear & conspicuous) #ad #sponsored or "I received this product for free"
Affiliate link (influencer gets commission) Disclosure required before link click #ad #affiliate or "I earn commission"
Testimonial doesn't reflect typical results Must be honest about results; cannot cherry-pick "Results may vary. See 50 reviews for typical experience."
🔶 Medium Risk: Undisclosed Influencer Deals

Scenario: You send product to 10 micro-influencers; they post gushing reviews without #ad disclosure. FTC sees pattern = warning letter to you + influencers + potential fine.

Mitigation: Influencer agreement template required. All influencers must disclose material connection prominently in first 3 words of post (before "read more").

III. Product Liability & Insurance

A. Types of Liability Exposure

Liability Type Scenario Exposure
Defective Product Bottle contains contamination or wrong formula = customer gets rash Medical bills + pain/suffering + punitive damages (up to 3x actual)
Failure to Warn Product causes allergic reaction; label doesn't warn about risk Claim for damages; negligence liability
Misrepresentation Claims you made are false; customer is injured Breach of warranty; fraud damages possible
Regulatory Violation FTC/FDA enforcement action for misleading claims Civil fines ($10k–100k+); corrective advertising costs

B. Insurance Requirements

Product Liability Insurance is non-negotiable. Cost: $1,200–3,000/year for $1M coverage. You must:

  • Obtain a $1M minimum General Liability + Product Liability policy
  • Verify coverage includes "Bodily Injury" + "Property Damage" + "Advertising Injury" (claims of false advertising)
  • Ensure coverage explicitly covers "cosmetic products"
  • Maintain policy continuously; lapse = uninsured liability exposure
🔴 Critical Risk: Uninsured Liability

Scenario: Customer alleges allergic reaction; medical bills are $50k. No insurance = you pay out-of-pocket. With one serious injury claim, your business is wiped out.

Mitigation: Insurance is mandatory before first sale. Get quotes from brokers in Phase 9. Budget $1.5–3k/year.

C. Adverse Event Monitoring & Reporting

You must establish a system to:

  • Capture complaints: All customer complaints (rash, allergic reaction, etc.) logged in a database with date, severity, customer info
  • Escalate immediately: Any serious adverse event → notify insurance, legal counsel, and (within 15 days) FDA
  • Investigate root cause: Was it product defect, user error, contamination, or allergic reaction?
  • Issue recalls if needed: If defect is widespread, you may need to recall product (announce on website, customer notification, FDA notification)
Example: If 5 customers report "rash after using serum" within 1 week, investigate immediately. Could be contamination batch or compatibility issue. Notify insurance; consider product hold pending investigation.

IV. Intellectual Property & Contracts

A. Brand Name & Trademark

You must conduct a trademark search before launching. Steps:

  1. Search USPTO.gov for identical or confusingly similar marks in Class 3 (cosmetics)
  2. Google search for competing brand names
  3. Domain name availability check
  4. If clear: File federal trademark application (cost: $250–500 + attorney $500–1,500) for your brand name

Why it matters: If someone else owns the trademark, you can be sued for infringement (cease-and-desist, damages, rebranding costs).

B. Supplier Contracts & Quality Agreements

Your contract with the cosmetic manufacturer must include:

  • Specification Document: Exact formula, ingredients, concentrations, pH, stability requirements
  • Quality Assurance: Testing protocols (microbial, stability, potency), CoAs (Certificates of Analysis) required per batch
  • Batch Records: Supplier must maintain records for 3+ years; you have right to audit
  • Liability Indemnity: Supplier indemnifies you for defects in their manufacturing (doesn't apply to your claims or packaging)
  • Confidentiality: Formula is proprietary; supplier cannot disclose or use elsewhere
  • Insurance Requirements: Supplier must carry product liability + general liability insurance

C. Website Terms of Service & Privacy Policy

Before launching, you must have:

  • Terms of Service: Disclaimers, return policy, limitation of liability, dispute resolution
  • Privacy Policy: How you collect/use customer data, CCPA compliance (California), email opt-out, data retention
  • Disclaimer of Medical Claims: "This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."

Cost to draft: $1,000–3,000 (attorney) or $200–500 (template + light review).

D. Employee & Contractor Agreements

If you hire staff, use:

  • Offer letter or employment agreement (clarify at-will, compensation, IP ownership)
  • IP Assignment Agreement (any work done for company is company property)
  • Confidentiality/Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA) for anyone with access to formula/strategy

VI. Ongoing Compliance Obligations

A. Annual Tasks

  • Renew Product Liability Insurance — Verify coverage is active; update as growth occurs
  • Review Claims & Marketing — Audit all ads, social, emails for unsubstantiated claims; collect proof of substantiation
  • Adverse Events Audit — Review all complaints received in past year; assess any trends or recall risks
  • Tax Filings — Ensure state/federal returns filed timely; sales tax compliance by state

B. Per-Batch Tasks (Supplier Handoff)

  • Receive Certificate of Analysis (CoA) from supplier confirming stability, microbial testing, ingredient specs
  • Inspect first units from batch for quality (packaging, fill level, consistency)
  • Store CoA & batch documentation for 3+ years (audit trail)

C. If You Receive a Complaint

  • Log complaint immediately (date, customer info, description, severity)
  • If serious (allergic reaction, injury): Notify insurance + legal counsel within 24 hours
  • Investigate root cause (product defect vs. user sensitivity vs. contamination)
  • Consider product hold or batch recall if needed
  • FTC/FDA notification within 15 days if serious adverse event

D. If FTC/FDA Sends a Letter

Do not ignore. Steps:

  1. Contact an attorney immediately (within 24 hours)
  2. Do NOT respond without legal counsel
  3. Gather all substantiation documents & claims files
  4. Attorney will draft response (if required) within the FTC's deadline (typically 10 business days)
  5. Be prepared for corrective advertising (you pay to remove misleading claims from media)

VIII. What NOT to Do

🔴 Critical: Absolute Prohibitions

DO NOT:

  • Make drug claims. "Stimulates collagen," "treats acne," "reduces wrinkles," "heals skin" = FDA drug classification + enforcement action.
  • Source injectable peptides. Peptides marketed for injection are drugs, not cosmetics. Do not touch. This is a felony in some jurisdictions.
  • Skip product liability insurance. One injury claim without insurance = bankruptcy risk.
  • Ignore adverse events. If someone reports an allergic reaction, log it, investigate, and report to FDA within 15 days. Hiding complaints = fraud (FTC can sue).
  • Use undisclosed influencers. Every influencer must disclose material connection (free product or commission). FTC actively polices this.
  • Make claims without substantiation. Have clinical data or published research on file before claiming any benefit.
  • Copy competitor claims without attribution. If you say "Clinically proven to improve fine lines," you must have your own study or cite the source.
  • Ignore FTC/FDA letters. Silence = admission of guilt. Get a lawyer immediately.
